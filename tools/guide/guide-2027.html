1. How to use this guide

Studying in Europe is very achievable from Southern Africa, but the students who succeed usually start early, budget honestly and get their documents right the first time.

Read chapters 2 and 3 first: your budget decides which countries are realistic. Then use the timeline in chapter 4 to plan backwards from your intake. Chapters 6 to 8 are your reference when you prepare your application and visa.

Figures change. Fees, visa costs and proof-of-funds amounts in this guide were checked in November 2026 against official and university sources. Governments update them often, so always confirm the current figure on the official website before you pay anything. Our destination pages at kuroeduconsultancy.com/destinations show the date each country was last verified.

This guide is free and you may share it. It is general information, not legal or immigration advice for your personal case.

If your budget is tight

Look first at Poland, Hungary, Latvia and Lithuania: low tuition and living costs, English-taught programmes, and the EU degree is recognised internationally. Germany is excellent if you can fund the blocked account.

If you want the shortest route

UK degrees are shorter, which lowers total living costs, but tuition and the health surcharge are high. Compare the total cost of the whole degree, not just one year.

Ask yourself: Can I pay the first year's tuition plus show the required proof of funds before my visa appointment? If not, choose a cheaper country or build in a scholarship plan.
Chapter 3

What it really costs

Your budget has two parts: what you will actually spend, and what the embassy wants to see in your account before it gives you a visa. They are not the same number.

Typical monthly spending

  • Accommodation, 40–50%: a dormitory or shared flat. Apply for a dorm as soon as you accept your offer; places go fast.
  • Food, 20–25%: cooking at home and using student canteens keeps this low.
  • Transport, about 5%: student discounts of 50% or more on city passes are common.
  • Health insurance: required for your visa or residence permit.
  • Phone, books and personal costs: budget €50–€100 a month.

Proof of funds: the legal minimums

CountryWhat you must show (2026 figures)
GermanyBlocked account with €992 per month, so €11,904 for 12 months
AustriaAbout €722.58 per month if under 24; €1,308.39 per month if 24 or older
LatviaRoughly €780 per month
Other countriesUsually 12 months of living costs plus the first year's tuition, shown in bank statements or a sponsor letter. Check the exact rule for your country of application.

One-off costs people forget

  • Visa or residence permit fees (chapter 7) and visa centre service fees
  • Certified copies, translations and apostille or legalisation of documents
  • English test fees, if your programme requires a test
  • University application fees and tuition deposits
  • Flights, first month's rent plus a deposit, and winter clothing
Rule of thumb: arrive with at least one extra month of living costs as an emergency fund. Many problems in the first weeks (delayed bank account, deposit for a room) are simply cash-flow problems.

Chapter 5

Entry requirements and English

Bachelor's degrees

You need a recognised school-leaving qualification: A-levels (ZIMSEC or Cambridge), the South African NSC/Matric, NSSC (Namibia), BGCSE (Botswana) or an equivalent. Requirements vary by university and subject. For example, the Polish consulate generally expects Zimbabwean undergraduate applicants to have at least 3 A-level passes. Science and engineering programmes usually want maths, and often physics or chemistry.

If your qualification is not accepted for direct entry, a foundation year can bridge the gap.

Master's degrees

A recognised bachelor's degree in a related field. Some programmes ask for a minimum grade, work experience or a portfolio.

English

  • Most English-taught programmes ask for IELTS 5.5–6.5 or an equivalent such as TOEFL or the Duolingo English Test.
  • Many universities accept an English Medium of Instruction (MOI) letter from your school or university if you studied in English.
  • Visa authorities can have their own English rules (the UK in particular), so check both the university and the visa requirement.
Recognition at home: if you plan to work in a regulated profession (medicine, nursing, law, engineering) back home, check before you apply that your home country's professional body recognises the degree.
Chapter 6

Your document checklist

Missing or badly prepared documents are the most common reason applications and visas are delayed. Start collecting these early.

For your university application

  • Passport (valid 12+ months beyond arrival)
  • School-leaving certificate and results
  • Transcripts; degree certificate for master's
  • Certified copies, translated if not in English
  • English test result or MOI letter
  • Motivation letter / statement of purpose
  • CV, and references if requested
  • Passport photos

For your visa or residence permit

  • Acceptance letter from the university
  • Proof of tuition payment or deposit
  • Proof of funds (bank statements, sponsor letter, or blocked account)
  • Health insurance for your stay
  • Proof of accommodation
  • Completed visa application form and fee
  • Apostilled or legalised documents where required
  • Biometrics appointment confirmation
Sponsor funding? Embassies look closely at where the money comes from. If a parent or relative is paying, prepare a signed sponsor letter, their ID, recent bank statements and proof of income. Large sudden deposits without explanation are a common red flag.
Chapter 7

The student visa, step by step

  1. Get your acceptance letter and pay any deposit your university requires.
  2. Check the official requirements for your country of residence on the embassy or immigration website.
  3. Book the appointment immediately. In peak season (May–August) slots can be weeks away.
  4. Prepare funds and documents exactly as listed; bring originals and copies.
  5. Attend the appointment for biometrics and sometimes a short interview about your studies and funding.
  6. Wait for the decision; do not book non-refundable flights before you have your visa.
  7. After arrival, register your address and collect or apply for your residence permit where required.

Chapter 8

Country visa notes

Poland

Apply for a national (Type D) visa at the Polish embassy or consulate responsible for your country after receiving your acceptance letter and paying your tuition deposit. Decisions usually take about 15 working days, up to 30 in complex cases. After arrival you can apply for a temporary residence permit for the length of your degree.

Germany

You need a national visa, a blocked account (€992/month for 2026) and health insurance. Processing is 6–12 weeks, so open the blocked account early. Many German-taught programmes require B2–C1 German.

Hungary

Student visa through a consular mission, then a residence permit. Stipendium Hungaricum scholars apply through their home country's partner ministry first.

Austria

For stays over 6 months you need a student residence permit applied for before travel. Register your address (Meldezettel) within 3 days of arrival.

Cyprus

Your university files the entry permit with the Migration Department while you are still outside Cyprus. You cannot switch to student status after entering as a tourist. Register within 7–10 days of arrival.

Latvia, Lithuania, Malta

Latvia: temporary residence permit from PMLP, usually through an embassy. Lithuania: start in the MIGRIS online system, then attend an appointment. Malta: national (Type D) visa, then an Identità residence permit after arrival.

Never travel on a tourist visa planning to switch to student status. Most countries do not allow it, and it can damage future applications.

How to stand out

  1. Start 12 months early: most deadlines fall 8–12 months before the course.
  2. Match the scholarship's purpose: development scholarships want to know how you will use the degree at home.
  3. Get strong references from people who know your work, and give them time.
  4. Apply to several: treat scholarships like university applications.

Working while you study, and after

Germany140 full days or 280 half days per year

AustraliaUp to 48 hours per fortnight in term, unlimited in breaks
UKUsually up to 20 hours a week in term for degree students
Other EU countriesCommonly around 20 hours a week in term; the exact limit is on your residence permit

Part-time work helps with costs and builds experience, but it cannot replace your proof of funds, and failing courses can put your residence permit at risk.

After graduation

Many destinations let graduates stay to look for work: the UK Graduate Route, Canada's Post-Graduation Work Permit, Australia's Temporary Graduate visa, and job-seeker residence permits in Germany and Poland. Lengths and conditions change, so check the rules in your final year.

Chapter 11

Arrival: your first 30 days

First week

  • Register your address where required (Austria: within 3 days)
  • Collect or apply for your residence permit; in Cyprus register within 7–10 days
  • Local SIM card
  • Attend university orientation

First month

  • Open a bank account
  • Confirm health insurance and register with a doctor if needed
  • Get a student transport pass
  • Find the African student association and international office

What surprises students most

  • Independent study: fewer contact hours, more reading and group work. Steady work beats cramming.
  • Winter: below freezing from December to February in Poland, Germany, Latvia and Lithuania. Buy a proper coat and boots locally.
  • Homesickness peaks in the first winter: keep a routine, stay in touch, and use your university's counselling service.
Chapter 12

Avoiding scams and costly mistakes

Every year students lose money to fake agents, fake accommodation and "guaranteed visa" promises. Protect yourself:

  • Nobody can guarantee a visa. Only the embassy decides. Walk away from anyone who promises otherwise.
  • Pay tuition to the university's own account, shown on its official website or invoice, never to a personal account.
  • Never pay a rental deposit before seeing a contract, and be wary of rooms that seem too cheap.
  • Check that the university and programme are accredited and listed on the official national register.
  • Never submit false documents or bank statements. It can lead to a refusal and a ban from future applications.
  • Keep copies of everything you submit and every receipt.
If an offer feels rushed ("pay today or lose your place"), slow down and verify with the university directly.
